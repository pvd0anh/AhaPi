## Ratios That Change

The trickiest ratio problems describe a situation **before** and **after** something happens. The quantities change, so the ratio changes too.

**Worked example.** A bag holds red and blue counters in the ratio $2:3$. There are 30 counters in total. If 6 red counters are added, what is the new ratio?

Start by finding the actual numbers.

$$\text{shares}=2+3=5 \qquad \text{one share}=\frac{30}{5}=6$$

$$\text{red}=12 \qquad \text{blue}=18$$

Now apply the change: red becomes $12+6=18$, blue stays $18$.

$$\text{new ratio}=18:18=1:1$$

> **The rule:** never try to change a ratio directly. Convert to real quantities first, apply the change, then rebuild the ratio and simplify.

## Simplifying the Final Answer

A ratio answer should normally be given in its simplest form — divide both parts by their highest common factor.

| Ratio | HCF | Simplest form |
|---|---|---|
| $18:18$ | 18 | $1:1$ |
| $12:30$ | 6 | $2:5$ |
| $21:14$ | 7 | $3:2$ |

[[EXERCISES]]

[[Q]] Apples and pears are in the ratio $3:4$, with 42 fruits in total. Five pears are eaten. What is the new ratio, in simplest form?
[[A]] One share $=\dfrac{42}{7}=6$, so apples $=18$ and pears $=24$. After eating 5 pears there are 19 left, giving $18:19$, which cannot be simplified.

[[Q]] A class has boys and girls in the ratio $5:4$, 36 students in total. Four more girls join. What is the new ratio?
[[A]] One share $=\dfrac{36}{9}=4$, so boys $=20$, girls $=16$. After 4 girls join: $20:20=1:1$.

[[Q]] Two jars hold sweets in the ratio $7:3$, and there are 40 sweets altogether. How many sweets must move from the first jar to the second so that both jars hold the same amount?
[[A]] One share $=\dfrac{40}{10}=4$, so the jars hold $28$ and $12$. Equal jars mean $20$ each, so **8 sweets** must move.
